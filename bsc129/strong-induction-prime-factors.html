Strong induction: why every n > 1 splits into primes

BSC129 Discrete Mathematics · Topic 4 Induction, Week 8 slide 14 (Fundamental theorem of arithmetic). Example: k + 1 = 12. Use the buttons or ← → keys.

P(n): n is a prime or a product of primes   (n ≥ 2)
Basis: P(2) is true, since 2 is prime.
Assume P(2), P(3), ..., P(k) are ALL true.
Show P(k+1):
  Case 1: k+1 is prime. Done.
  Case 2: k+1 = a · b, with 2 ≤ a ≤ b ≤ k.
    By the hypothesis, a and b are primes
    or products of primes,
    so k+1 = a · b is a product of primes.
By strong induction, P(n) holds for all n ≥ 2.

Why strong induction: 12 = 3 · 4 uses 3 and 4, not 11. Ordinary induction only gives P(k); strong induction gives every P(2) ... P(k).

Always split into two cases: k+1 is prime, or k+1 = a · b. An answer with only one case is incomplete.

Say why the hypothesis applies: write 2 ≤ a ≤ b ≤ k, so a and b are inside the range the hypothesis covers.

Existence only: this proof shows a factorisation exists; that it is unique is a separate proof.
